01 · The drop

Growth fell by almost half

What happened in Washington after the federal EV tax credit ended last September? Battery-electric growth fell by almost half, from about 3,600 new BEVs a month to about 1,800.

02 · Who slowed

The slowdown was not shared equally

About 90% of the lost additions came from the five largest counties, led by King. Those five kept only 45% of their earlier pace. Seven mid-sized counties, led by Benton, kept 91%.

03 · Still growing

Slower, but still growing

BEVs were still added in every period: about 2,000–2,500 a month after the first drop. The Aug–Oct dip came as all passenger cars fell by about 49,000 a month, so it looks like a market-wide slump, not a collapse in BEV demand.

04 · The problem

One measure always misses someone

Those five large counties still receive about 70% of new BEVs, so if we rank counties only by vehicles added, we miss places like Benton that are still growing fast. If we rank only by growth rate, tiny counties like Garfield, which added just four cars, jump to the top. Either way, we miss someone.

05 · Both measures

Seven counties pass both tests

So I used both measures. Seven counties are above the median on both vehicles added and growth rate.

06 · Where they are

Three groups, three places

The five largest sit around Puget Sound and Clark County. The seven growing counties are split between the Columbia Basin and the southwest. Fast-growing counties with few BEVs sit mostly in the east.

07 · Looking ahead

Demand grows either way

At the current pace, Washington will add about 88,000 more BEVs by 2030. If a change in administration brings the tax credit back in 2029, and additions in the final year recover to their earlier pace, the total increase would be 24% higher than the baseline. Charging demand would grow under either scenario, so we should not wait.

08 · The ask

Two surveys by Feb 28, 2027

I ask the clean-transportation office to do two things by February 28, 2027. First, survey charging access in the seven growing counties. Second, review the capacity and use of existing chargers in the five largest counties. Then use the findings to allocate the charging budget.

09 · Who gets left out

Don't forget rural counties

Finally, please do not forget rural counties left out by our criteria. Starting with eastern Washington, survey the distance to the nearest public charger and the needs of households that cannot charge at home. Please also discuss support tailored to rural counties, including help with home charger installation costs.
